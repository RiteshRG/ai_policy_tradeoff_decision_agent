# Decision Report

## 1. Problem / Scenario
- India plans to meet its E20 ethanol‑blending target (20 % ethanol in petrol). Two pathways are considered: **Rapid Expansion** (quickly scale ethanol supply) vs **Gradual Adoption** (phase‑in over several years). The decision must balance food & water security, energy security, and vehicle/consumer impacts.

## 2. Policy Options
- **Rapid Expansion** – Accelerate ethanol production to hit the 2025‑26 target, using current agricultural capacity and incentives.
- **Gradual Adoption** – Extend the timeline, allowing supply‑side adjustments and technology upgrades before full E20 rollout.

## 4. Evidence Summary
### Food & Water Security
- Ethanol feedstock (sugarcane, maize, rice‑straw) pulls acreage from pulses and millet, raising food‑price volatility. (Food & Water file)
- Sugarcane’s high water demand deepens groundwater depletion in stressed basins. (Food & Water file)
- Some income gains and reduced stubble‑burning are noted, plus modest drip‑irrigation adoption. (Food & Water file)
### Energy Security
- E20 cuts crude‑oil import bills by $10‑12 bn annually but supplies only 0.3‑0.4 % of total primary energy. (Energy file)
- Domestic ethanol production meets 70‑80 % of volume, improving fuel mix resilience. (Energy file)
- Seasonal supply constraints limit consistent import substitution. (Energy file)
### Vehicle & Consumer Impact
- Modern vehicles see 2‑4 % mileage loss; older engines suffer 5‑8 % loss and potential component wear. (Vehicle file)
- Net consumer cost rise ≈1‑2 % per kilometre for older vehicles; negligible for compliant models. (Vehicle file)
- Emissions drop up to 5 % for newer engines, stabilising fuel supply chains. (Vehicle file)

## 5. Conflicts / Disagreements
- **Energy lens** favors Rapid Expansion (score 7) for immediate import‑bill savings.
- **Food/Water** and **Vehicle/Consumer** lenses favor Gradual Adoption (scores 7, 7) due to lower water stress, less food‑crop displacement, and fewer consumer cost penalties.
- The divergence means the energy gain of rapid rollout must be weighed against longer‑term sustainability and consumer burdens.

## 7. Trade‑off Analysis
- **Rapid Expansion** – Advantage: Immediate foreign‑exchange savings and fuel‑mix diversification. Disadvantage: Heightened water stress and higher consumer costs for older vehicles.
- **Gradual Adoption** – Advantage: Preserves water resources, protects food‑crop acreage, and limits mileage penalties. Disadvantage: Delays energy‑security gains and fiscal savings.

## 8. Recommendation
**Recommended Option: Gradual Adoption** – It scores higher overall, aligns with food‑water sustainability, and minimizes consumer hardship while still progressing toward E20.

## 9. Confidence & Uncertainty
- Confidence: **Medium** – Evidence is recent but quantitative trade‑offs (e.g., exact water savings) are uncertain.
- Key uncertainties: future ethanol yield improvements, policy incentives for water‑efficient farming, and speed of vehicle fleet turnover.
- Caveats: assumes current technology trends continue; abrupt policy shifts could alter scores.

## 10. Decision Conditions
- Recommendation holds if water‑stress regions remain vulnerable and older vehicle stock stays >30 % of fleet.
- If ethanol‑yield technologies double or fuel‑price premiums shrink, rapid rollout may become preferable.
- Significant government subsidies for sustainable feedstock could also shift the balance.
