Lens Question: How does India's E20 ethanol‑blending programme affect vehicle performance and consumers?

Key Findings:
- Nationwide testing by ARAI (40‑60 k km runs) shows modern (post‑2015) cars and motorcycles meet durability standards with no measurable engine‑wear issues, though fuel‑economy drops 2‑4 % on average.
- SIAM data confirm the 2‑4 % mileage loss, but older gasoline engines (pre‑2010) and many two‑wheelers experience larger declines (5‑8 %) and occasional fuel‑system corrosion due to ethanol‑sensitive seals and fuel‑pump materials.
- The lower energy density of ethanol (≈ 30 MJ kg⁻¹ vs ≈ 44 MJ kg⁻¹ for petrol) means the same litre delivers less kinetic energy; newer engine control units (ECUs) compensate partially, while older ECUs cannot, leading to higher specific fuel consumption.
- Consumer‑level costs rise modestly: a 3 % mileage drop offsets the typical 5‑10 % price premium of E20, yielding a net cost increase of ~1‑2 % per kilometre for older vehicles, and negligible change for compliant newer models.
- No widespread warranty claims have been reported for OEM‑certified E20‑compatible models, indicating that manufacturers’ compatibility assurances hold for the majority of the fleet.

Positive Impacts:
- New‑generation engines gain up to 5 % reduction in CO₂ per kilometre thanks to the renewable ethanol fraction, supporting emission‑norm compliance.
- E20 availability stabilises fuel supply chains, reducing price volatility for consumers with compliant vehicles.

Negative Impacts:
- Older vehicles incur higher fuel consumption and may face accelerated wear of rubber fuel‑system components, prompting possible retrofits or earlier replacement.
- Consumers must navigate mixed‑fuel availability (E20 vs. E10/XP95) and may incur extra costs for additives or fuel‑filter changes.

Lens Assessment: E20 delivers modest emissions benefits with negligible engine impact for modern, certified vehicles, but imposes measurable mileage penalties and maintenance concerns for older, non‑compatible cars and two‑wheelers, translating into modest additional consumer costs.
